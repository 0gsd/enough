# Research Strategy & Entry Templates

## Research Philosophy

The goal is not to replicate an encyclopedia. The goal is to hand a historical fiction author the *specific, surprising, connective material* that transforms a story from "set in the past" to "alive in the past." 

An author can Google the basics. What they can't easily find:
- The name of the specific wine served at a particular court
- That two historical figures were in the same city at the same time and almost certainly crossed paths
- That a technology we associate with one era was actually invented decades earlier but suppressed
- What the streets smelled like in a particular season
- The slang a dockworker would have used vs. a merchant vs. a priest

That's what wiki-links is for.

## Search Methodology

### The Spiral Technique

Start at the center (the core topic) and spiral outward:

**Ring 1 — The Facts**: Get the core event right. Dates, key players, sequence of events. Verify against multiple sources.

**Ring 2 — The Context**: What else was happening? Same city, same year. What was the political situation? Economic conditions? Was there a war, a famine, a boom?

**Ring 3 — The People**: Who was there but isn't famous for being there? Servants, merchants, engineers, translators, spies. The people who don't get chapters in history books but who would be *characters* in a novel.

**Ring 4 — The Senses**: What did it look, sound, smell, taste, feel like? Period-specific fabrics, building materials, cooking methods, lighting, transport, sanitation. The stuff that makes a reader *believe* they're there.

**Ring 5 — The Ironies**: What do we know now that they didn't? What were they wrong about? What nearly happened but didn't? What happened by accident? These are the narrative engines.

### Search Query Patterns

Good queries are specific and historically grounded:

| Intent | Bad Query | Good Query |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Daily life | "medieval daily life" | "London 1380s merchant daily routine" |
| Food | "ancient Roman food" | "Pompeii thermopolium menu archaeological" |
| Minor figures | "people at [event]" | "[event] eyewitness accounts letters" |
| Technology | "Renaissance inventions" | "Florence 1490s workshop tools techniques" |
| Sensory | "what did [place] look like" | "[place] [year] traveler description contemporary account" |
| Misconceptions | "[topic] myths" | "[topic] common misconceptions historians debunked" |

### Following Threads

When a search result mentions an interesting name, place, or detail:
1. Note it
2. Search for it specifically
3. Check if it connects to anything else you've found
4. If it connects to 2+ other entries, it belongs in the web

This is the "wiki-links" behavior — following hyperlinks through history.

## Entry Template

Each entry in the web follows this structure:

```
### [Entry Title]
**Type**: [Person | Event | Place | Object | Cultural Detail | Technology | Custom]
**When**: [Date or date range]
**Where**: [Location]

[2-4 paragraphs of content: who/what this is, why it matters, what makes it interesting for fiction. Include specific details — names, numbers, physical descriptions, quotes from primary sources where available.]

**Wiki-links**: → [Entry A], → [Entry B], → [Entry C]
[1-2 sentences explaining HOW this entry connects to the linked entries. These connections are the value — they show the author how to weave these elements together.]

**Story seed**: [1-2 sentences suggesting how a fiction author might use this — a scene idea, a character trait, a plot mechanism, a telling detail.]

**Confidence**: [HIGH | MEDIUM | LOW] — How well-sourced is this entry?
```

### Entry Type Guidelines

**Person**: Include physical description if available, social position, daily concerns, relationships to other entries. Avoid just listing accomplishments — focus on what they *were like*.

**Event**: Include specific timing (time of day if known), weather, crowd size, immediate reactions. The cinematic details.

**Place**: Describe it as a set designer would need it. Dimensions, materials, lighting, sounds, what's adjacent to it.

**Object**: What does it look like? Feel like? How is it made? Who touches it? Where does it live?

**Cultural Detail**: Customs, rituals, habits, taboos, fashions, entertainments. The social operating system of the era.

**Technology**: How does it actually work? Who operates it? What does it replace? What does it sound like?

## Cross-Reference Strategy

The "wiki-links" between entries should reveal non-obvious connections:

**Strong connections** (use these):
- "X was in the same city as Y at the same time, and both frequented Z"
- "The technology described in Entry A was used to build the structure in Entry B"  
- "The cultural practice in Entry C would have been observed by the person in Entry D"
- "Entry E happened *because* of Entry F, though historians only realized this recently"

**Weak connections** (avoid these):
- "Both happened in the same century"
- "Both involve water/food/politics" (too generic)
- "Both are interesting" (not a connection)

The goal: an author should be able to pick any entry, follow its wiki-links, and find themselves building a scene that is richer than anything they'd have written from the core topic alone.

## Author's Toolkit Guidelines

### Sensory Palette
Not "it smelled bad." Instead: "The tanneries along the south bank produced a sharp, ammoniac reek from the urine vats used to cure leather, strongest in summer, that mixed with the coal smoke from the smithies into something residents called 'the London perfume.'"

Every sense. Specific to the era and place. Cite sources where possible.

### Vocabulary
Not just fancy old words. Include:
- **Trade jargon**: What did workers in the relevant industries call their tools and processes?
- **Social markers**: Words that indicated class, region, education
- **Period slang**: Informal language, insults, endearments
- **Technical terms**: The specific names for things we'd now describe generically

Format: **word** (pronunciation if non-obvious) — definition and usage context

### Common Misconceptions
These are gold for fiction because they let the author demonstrate authentic knowledge. "Actually, in 1850 London, most people DID bathe regularly — the myth of universal medieval filth is largely Victorian propaganda."

### Narrative Hooks
Frame these as "what if" prompts:
- "What if your character was one of the 200 people who witnessed [specific event] and noticed [specific detail] that historians now think was the key to [consequence]?"
- "The three-day gap in [historical figure]'s diary in [month/year] has never been explained. Your character could fill it."
- "The [object] that was lost during [event] was never recovered. Where did it go?"

## Output Format

The final dossier should be clean markdown:

```markdown
# Wiki-Links: [Topic]
## Research Dossier for Historical Fiction

**Generated for**: [topic as provided]
**Focus**: [focus if specified, otherwise "General"]  
**Depth**: [Summary | Deep]
**Era radius**: [as specified or default ±10 years]

---

## I. Core Brief

[3-8 paragraphs depending on depth]

---

## II. The Web

[5-10 entries using the entry template above]

---

## III. Author's Toolkit

### Sensory Palette
[5-8 sensory details]

### Vocabulary
[8-12 terms with definitions]

### Common Misconceptions
[2-4 debunked myths]

### Narrative Hooks
[3-5 story seeds]

---

*Research conducted via web search. Claims marked [UNVERIFIED] require additional verification. All other claims verified against at least one source.*
```
