# Follow-ups — the pass to the user

This step is not optional and it is not long. Three to six questions, asked
of the **user**, after the answers are in and before anything is drafted.

## Why it exists

The respondent answered questions about how they think. They could not answer
the questions that matter most to the finished readvisor, because those are
not about them:

- What this user needs from this person's judgment.
- Where that judgment has actually been right, and where it has not.
- What the user wants argued with.
- What tone they want it taken in.

A readvisor built from the answers alone is a portrait. A readvisor built
from the answers *and* this pass is something the user will switch on again
next month. This is the step that makes the difference, and it takes ten
minutes.

Ask one or two at a time, the same as everything else. Save the answers to
`rness/io/output/readvisory/<name>/context.md`.

---

## The five questions

Ask all five when the source is somebody else. Add the sixth if the user is
still talking.

### 1. How do you know this person's judgment?

> "How long have you known how she thinks about this, and where did you see
> it? I'm after the setting — watching her work, being on the receiving end,
> hearing her talk about other people's."

**Why:** it tells you which parts of the returned answers to weight. Somebody
who watched this person decide things for ten years knows something the
questionnaire cannot contain.

**Feeds:** the opening paragraph and *Core orientation* in `AGENT.md`;
*Reading of the profile* in `MOTIVATION.md`.

### 2. Where has it been right, and where has it been wrong?

> "When has her judgment on this turned out to be right in a way that
> surprised you? And is there a time it was wrong — or right about the
> subject and wrong about you?"

**Why:** this is the single most valuable question in the skill. The "wrong"
half is what keeps the finished readvisor from being a fan letter, and it is
usually where the user is most specific.

**Feeds:** *Likely failure modes* and *Notes for the user* in
`MOTIVATION.md`; the low tier of *How to apply this profile*.

If the user cannot think of a wrong, push once, gently: *"Not wrong exactly —
but a kind of question where you'd take her answer and then go and check?"*
Then let it go.

### 3. What do you want to be challenged on?

> "What do you want this readvisor to argue with you about? What's the thing
> you'd rather it didn't let you get away with?"

**Why:** a readvisor that only agrees is furniture. This answer becomes an
explicit instruction in `AGENT.md`, in the readvisor's own voice.

**Feeds:** *Anti-patterns* and *Core moves*.

### 4. Where should it defer?

> "Where should this one hand you over — to another readvisor, to a real
> person, or to sleeping on it?"

**Why:** the user knows their own council. The source's own limits (LIMIT-3)
come first, but the user's answer here is what makes a hand-off *usable*:
"hand this to open-skeptic" is better advice than "ask somebody else".

**Feeds:** *Hand-offs*.

### 5. What tone do you want from it?

> "When you're stuck and you ask it something, how do you want to be spoken
> to? Blunt, patient, funny, brisk? And is that how she actually is, or is
> that what you need?"

**Why:** tone is the difference between a readvisor the user opens and one
they close. The second half of the question matters: sometimes the user wants
the person's judgment in a voice unlike the person's, and it is much better
to know that now than to be corrected three drafts later.

**Feeds:** *Tone notes* and *Communication style*.

### 6. Optional — what will you ask it first?

> "What's the first question you're actually going to bring it?"

**Why:** it is the fastest way to build the *How to apply this profile*
section, because it is a real question rather than a category. Ask it when
the brief was vague or when the user is enjoying themselves.

---

## What a good answer looks like

> **User:** "Right about structure basically always. She'd read something of
> mine and say 'this starts on page three' and she'd be right and I'd be
> annoyed for a day. Wrong about how much people would care — she thought
> the technical stuff was a turn-off and it turned out to be the reason
> anybody read it at all."

That is two sentences and it gives you: a core move (*finds where the piece
actually starts*), a quotable phrase (*"this starts on page three"*), a
failure mode (*under-rates a technical reader's appetite*), and the tone
(*right and annoying, and the user does not mind*).

Compare:

> **User:** "She's a really good editor."

That is nothing. Follow it: *"Good at what, specifically — what does she
catch that other people don't?"*

## The live variant — contradiction and stress

When the user interviewed *themselves* in step 2, four of the five questions
above collapse (they cannot tell you how they know their own judgment). The
slot stays; the questions change.

Ask 3–6 of these, built from what they actually said:

**1. The contradiction.** Find two answers that pull against each other and
put them side by side.

> "Earlier you said the first thing you cut under a deadline is polish. Later
> you said the thing you will not give up is the last read-through. When does
> each one win?"

This is the highest-signal question in the whole skill. The answer is almost
always the sentence you end up quoting in *Core orientation*. If there is no
contradiction in the answers, the interview was too shallow — ask two more
questions from the bank before you draft.

**2. The self-rebuttal.** `REB-2` from the bank, aimed at something they
said.

> "Make the strongest case for the opposite of what you told me about cutting
> projects early. Try to make it sound right."

**3. The suspect advice.** `REB-3`.

> "What's a piece of advice you give regularly that you half-suspect is
> wrong?"

**4. The limit.** The self-interview version of the hand-offs question.

> "When should this readvisor *not* be trusted — including about things you
> genuinely know well?"

**5. The tone.** Question 5 above, unchanged. It applies either way.

> "When you're stuck at eleven at night and you ask this thing something, how
> do you want to be spoken to?"

**6. The distance.** The question that makes a self-interview useful at all.

> "This readvisor is going to be you, minus the day you're having. What
> should it hold on to that you drop when you're tired or discouraged?"

Save all of it to `context.md` the same way.

## The shape of context.md

Plain, short, and readable by a session that has never met this user:

    # Context — <Display Name>

    Answers from the user, <date>. Source of the profile: <the user
    themselves | somebody else, by questionnaire>.

    ## How the user knows this judgment
    ## Where it has been right
    ## Where it has been wrong
    ## What the user wants argued with
    ## Where it should defer
    ## Tone the user asked for
    ## Notes

    <Anything else the user said that you will want when drafting —
    especially exact phrases. Quote them.>

For the live variant, replace the first three headings with
`## Contradictions and how they resolve`, `## Where it should not be
trusted`, and `## What it should hold on to`.

Quote the user directly wherever they were vivid. Their phrasings end up in
the finished documents just as often as the source's do, and by the time you
are drafting you will not remember which was which unless you wrote it down.

---

## Then

Go to `synthesis.md`. Do not draft anything before this pass is finished —
the sections it feeds are exactly the ones that are hardest to retrofit.
