# Question bank

Every question a readvisory session can draw from, grouped by axis. A session
uses **12–18 of them**. The bank is deliberately small: it is a pool to choose
from, not a list to get through.

Use `coverage.md` to decide which ones. Use this file to get the wording right.

## Markers

- `[FC]` — forced choice. Give the lettered options. Always follow with *why*.
- `[RANK]` — ranking. Give the items, ask for an order.
- `[FREE]` — free response. Cap it at about three sentences.
- `[APH]` — aphorism reaction. Give the saying with no attribution. Ask: true
  and important / true but overrated / a way to dodge specifics / cliché —
  and why.
- `[SCEN]` — a small abstract scenario about somebody who is not them.

The forced-choice style is doing real work. A blank "how do you make
decisions?" gets a blank answer. Four bad options get a real one, because the
person has to reject three things they half-believe.

**The why is the answer.** The letter is scaffolding. Every `[FC]` and
`[RANK]` is followed by "why?" — in a questionnaire, print that on the page.

## 0. Brief-shaping — ask the USER, before choosing anything else

Four questions, asked in step 1. They turn "I want a readvisor" into a subject
this bank can actually aim at. Ask two or three; stop when the subject is
sharp.

**BRIEF-1. [FREE] The recurring question.** What is the question you keep
having that you wish you could ask someone about? Say it the way you would
say it at the time, not the tidy version.

**BRIEF-2. [FC] What do you want back from it?**
- (a) A judgment — tell me what you'd do
- (b) A push — argue with what I've already decided
- (c) A diagnosis — tell me what kind of problem this is
- (d) A witness — help me think out loud without steering me

**BRIEF-3. [FREE] The last time.** Think of the last time you actually asked
this person (or yourself) this kind of question. What did they say, and was
it right?

**BRIEF-4. [FC] When should it stay quiet?** What kind of question should
this readvisor *decline* and hand back to you or to another readvisor?
- (a) Anything factual or technical
- (b) Anything about other people's feelings
- (c) Anything where the stakes are money or health
- (d) Nothing — it should always have a go
- (e) Something else (their words)

Take the answers into the session. BRIEF-1 sets the craft axis. BRIEF-2 sets
the tone of the finished readvisor. BRIEF-4 is the first draft of the
hand-offs section, in the user's own words.

## 1. Risk — how they move when the path is unclear

**RISK-1. [FC] Default move under uncertainty.** When the way forward isn't
clear, what do you do first?
- (a) Gather more information
- (b) Place a small bet you can learn from
- (c) Commit fully and adjust as you go
- (d) Wait for the situation to clarify itself

**RISK-2. [FC] The feedback loop you trust.** Which length of loop tells you
the truth most reliably?
- (a) Hours (b) Days (c) Weeks (d) Months (e) Years (f) Decades

**RISK-3. [FC] Baseline.** Most situations are:
- (a) Recoverable
- (b) Shaped by momentum
- (c) One decision away from a different outcome
- (d) Mostly determined by who is in the room

**RISK-4. [FREE] The bet you'd make again.** Describe — with no names or
specifics — a chance you took that did not work out, and say whether you
would take it again.

## 2. Method — how they take a problem apart

**METHOD-1. [FC] Decomposition.** Facing a tangled problem, do you:
- (a) Name the smallest piece you can unblock
- (b) Sketch the whole shape and look for the keystone
- (c) Talk it through until structure appears
- (d) Wait for someone else to frame it first

**METHOD-2. [FC] Under a deadline, what gets cut first?**
- (a) Scope (b) Polish (c) Talking to other people (d) Rest

**METHOD-3. [FC] What actually changes someone's mind?**
- (a) Evidence
- (b) Living through it
- (c) Watching someone they respect change theirs
- (d) Time

**METHOD-4. [FC] Two things you value genuinely conflict. Your move:**
- (a) Pick one and own the cost
- (b) Find a third frame
- (c) Put the question down for now
- (d) Let the situation decide

**METHOD-5. [FREE] The first question.** When someone brings you a problem,
what is the first question you ask them — the one you almost always ask?

---

## 3. Judgment — whose word they take, and why

**JUDGE-1. [FC] Whose call do you trust on a hard decision?**
- (a) The person closest to the work
- (b) The person with the longest view
- (c) The person with the most at stake
- (d) The person with the cleanest model of it

**JUDGE-2. [FC] Given a confident answer, your first private check is:**
- (a) What is their record on this kind of question?
- (b) What would have to be true for them to be wrong?
- (c) Who disagrees, and why?
- (d) Does the confidence match the difficulty?

**JUDGE-3. [FC] How far should expertise travel outside its own subject?**
- (a) A long way — sharp minds transfer
- (b) Some way — depends on the subject
- (c) Barely — context does most of the work
- (d) Almost not at all — off their own ground, experts are often worse than
  beginners

**JUDGE-4. [FREE] One sentence: what does somebody have to do, in front of
you, for you to stop checking their reasoning so hard?

---

## 4. Failure — what goes wrong, and what they watch for

**FAIL-1. [FC] When a good plan fails, the usual reason is:**
- (a) The world changed
- (b) The plan was wrong about people
- (c) Execution wandered
- (d) Somebody wasn't honest

**FAIL-2. [FC] When you have moved too fast and broken something, the cause
was usually:**
- (a) Skipped the boring step
- (b) Didn't bring in the right person
- (c) Trusted memory instead of checking
- (d) Confused urgency with importance

**FAIL-3. [FREE] The early sign.** What is the first thing you notice when
something is quietly going wrong — before anybody says so?

**FAIL-4. [FREE] The wrong post-mortem.** When something goes wrong, what do
people usually blame that was not actually the cause?

## 5. Taste — what "good" looks like to them

Taste questions are not decoration. They tell you how this person will
*frame* advice, which shows up in every line of the finished readvisor.

**TASTE-1. [RANK] Four cafés.** You are sending a friend to one. Put them in
the order you would recommend, and say why the first is first:
- the precise and quiet one
- the warm and chaotic one
- the fast and cheap one
- the slow and rare one

**TASTE-2. [FC] Something is well made when:**
- (a) It gets out of your way
- (b) It teaches you something
- (c) It makes you feel cared for
- (d) It makes the next thing easier

**TASTE-3. [FREE] An everyday object you have owned for years and would not
replace. What makes it right? *(No brand, no model — just the quality.)*

**TASTE-4. [FC] If a piece of writing is *too clean*, what is missing?**
- (a) Real friction
- (b) The writer's actual voice
- (c) An unanswered question
- (d) Anything that could be wrong

**TASTE-5. [FC] In writing you genuinely like reading, the most reliable
signal is:**
- (a) It makes specific claims you could disagree with
- (b) The sentences have rhythm
- (c) It earns its length
- (d) It changes how you see something you already knew

## 6. Voice — how they would actually say it to you

**VOICE-1. [FREE] First instinct.** A smart, anxious friend asks whether to
leave something safe for something risky. Knowing nothing else, your first
instinct is to encourage / discourage / ask more / just sit with them — and
why?

**VOICE-2. [FC] Thirty seconds, and the listener is distracted. You:**
- (a) Give the shortest version of the answer
- (b) Tell a small story
- (c) Ask one clarifying question
- (d) Wait until you have their attention

**VOICE-3. [FREE] "It depends."** When the honest answer is *it depends*,
what do you do next, most often?

**VOICE-4. [FC] How much of good advice is the wording, and how much the
substance?**
- (a) Almost all substance; wording barely matters
- (b) About 70/30, substance to wording
- (c) About half and half
- (d) The wording is most of the work

## 7. Conflict — disagreement, and who gets to be right

**CONF-1. [FC] A disagreement is heating up. Your instinct is:**
- (a) Name what is happening in the room
- (b) Push through to a decision
- (c) Change the subject and come back later
- (d) Leave and write it down

**CONF-2. [FC] You disagree with somebody who outranks you. Your default:**
- (a) Raise it privately once, then defer
- (b) Push until one of you is persuaded
- (c) Find allies first, then move together
- (d) Put your view on the record and get on with it

**CONF-3. [SCEN] Somebody you like is about to make a decision you think is
wrong, but not disastrous. Do you (a) say something, (b) let them learn it,
(c) say something only if asked, or (d) say something *and* offer to help
with the consequences? Why?

## 8. Craft — the subject this readvisor is actually for

**This axis has no fixed questions.** The brief names a subject; you write
two or three questions about *that*. These are the highest-signal questions
in the session, because they are the ones only this person can answer.

Compose them in the house style: short, forced-choice or capped free
response, always with a why. Three reliable patterns:

1. **The diagnosis question.** "When somebody brings you X, what are the two
   or three *kinds* of X you find yourself sorting them into?" — This one
   question often produces the readvisor's core move on its own.
2. **The threshold question.** "How do you know when X is finished / far
   enough / not worth continuing?"
3. **The failure question.** "What is the most common wrong thing people do
   about X, and what are they usually getting right when they do it?"

Worked examples, so the pattern is clear:

| Brief | Craft questions |
|---|---|
| *"Is this draft finished?"* | **[FC]** A draft that isn't working is usually: (a) not finished, (b) not started — the real piece is underneath it, (c) finished and being fiddled with, (d) the wrong length. · **[FREE]** What do you read first when somebody hands you a draft — the opening, the ending, or somewhere in the middle — and what are you checking? · **[FREE]** What do you say to somebody who cannot stop revising? |
| *"When do I cut a project?"* | **[FC]** A project worth abandoning usually shows it first by: (a) the work getting harder, (b) you stopping talking about it, (c) the reason for it changing, (d) other people losing interest. · **[FREE]** Name one thing you kept going with longer than you should have, and the sign you ignored. |
| *"How do I say a hard thing at work?"* | **[FC]** The hard part of a hard conversation is usually: (a) the opening sentence, (b) staying in the room after it, (c) knowing what you actually want, (d) the other person's audience. · **[FREE]** What is the sentence you reach for when somebody gets defensive? |

Ask at least **two** craft questions in every session. A readvisor built from
general questions alone gives general advice, which nobody needs.

## 9. Limits — what this readvisor should not do

These come near the end. They are the fastest route to an honest hand-offs
section, and people answer them well because they are being asked to be
careful with somebody they like.

**LIMIT-1. [FREE] Anything a readvisor built from your judgment should
**never** do?

**LIMIT-2. [FREE] Anything it should **always** do?

**LIMIT-3. [FREE] What kind of question should it hand back — "go ask
somebody who actually knows about this", or "sleep on it for a week"?

**LIMIT-4. [FREE] One thing you wish more people understood about this
subject — said as plainly as you can.

**LIMIT-5. [FREE] The best advice anybody ever gave you, in the words they
used. *(Leave their name out.)*

## 10. Stress — for testing a profile that is already forming

Use these only when the picture is clear enough to test. In a live session,
two or three near the end. In a questionnaire, at most one — you will not be
there to follow up.

### Aphorisms

Give the saying, no attribution, and ask: true and important / true but
overrated / a way to dodge specifics / cliché — and why.

- **APH-1.** "Move fast and break things."
- **APH-2.** "Premature optimization is the root of all evil."
- **APH-3.** "Slow is smooth, smooth is fast."
- **APH-4.** "The best is the enemy of the good."
- **APH-5.** "Hope is not a strategy."
- **APH-6.** "Disagree and commit."

### Scenarios

- **SCEN-1.** A friend's small project is failing slowly. They have enough
  left to keep trying for six more months and they are asking you. First
  instinct?
- **SCEN-2.** Something you care about is heading for a slow, predictable
  failure. Everyone seems to know and nobody is saying it. Your move?
- **SCEN-3.** Halfway through a piece of work you realise the assumption
  underneath it was wrong. The deadline has not moved. What do you do?

### Self-rebuttal — the highest-signal questions in the bank

- **REB-1.** Earlier you said *[their tendency, in their words]*. When is the
  opposite the right call?
- **REB-2.** Make the strongest case for the position opposite to the one you
  took on *[an earlier answer]*. Try to make it sound right.
- **REB-3.** What is a piece of advice you give regularly that you secretly
  suspect might be wrong?

REB-1 and REB-2 must be customised to something the person actually said.
Asked generically they are worthless; asked specifically they produce the
sentence you will end up quoting in `AGENT.md`.

## Writing a question the bank doesn't have

You will need to. Keep to the house style:

- **Short, and about the work, not the person.** "When does X become a
  problem?" beats "Is X good or bad?"
- **Forced choice with 2–4 options, or a free response capped at three
  sentences.** Never an open essay prompt.
- **Always pair it with a why.** The why is the answer.
- **No leading options.** If one choice is obviously the good one, the
  question is broken. Every option should be something a reasonable person
  actually believes.
- **Nothing that needs a life story to answer.** Ask about the pattern, not
  the episode.
- **Nothing from the never-ask list** in `privacy.md`.

Before you send it, read it once as the person receiving it. If it feels like
a personality test, an appraisal, or a job interview, throw it out and write
a smaller one.
