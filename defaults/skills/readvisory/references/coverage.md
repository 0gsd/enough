# Coverage — which question to ask next

A checklist, not an algorithm. Keep the map below in your working notes and
update it after every answer.

---

## The map

Nine axes. They are the section names in `question-bank.md`, so the map and
the bank line up one to one.

    [ ] craft      what this readvisor is actually for
    [ ] method     how they take a problem apart
    [ ] limits     what they would refuse or hand off
    [ ] risk       how they move when the path is unclear
    [ ] failure    what goes wrong, and what they watch for
    [ ] judgment   whose word they take, and why
    [ ] voice      how they would say it to you
    [ ] taste      what "good" looks like to them
    [ ] conflict   disagreement, and who gets to be right

Three states, and only three:

- `[ ]` **empty** — nothing yet.
- `[~] ` **one answer** — you have a letter, or a sentence, but you could not
  yet write a line of the readvisor from it.
- `[x]` **clear** — you could write a sentence about this person and point at
  the answer it came from.

Do not score anything out of ten. Do not track twenty axes. Nine boxes and
three states is what a session can actually hold.

---

## The target

A finished session looks like this:

- **The first three axes — craft, method, limits — are `[x]`.** These are
  load-bearing. A readvisor missing any of them is vague, generic, or
  overconfident about things it should hand off.
- **Craft has at least two answers.** Preferably three.
- **At least six of the nine are `[~]` or better.**
- **Two or three axes are deep** — `[x]` with a quotable sentence attached.

Axes still empty at the end are not a failure. They become honest silences in
the finished documents, and `MOTIVATION.md` says which ones they were.

---

## Choosing the next question — live sessions

Go down this list. **Stop at the first line that applies** and ask that
question.

1. **Is craft, method or limits still `[ ]`?** → Ask there. (Craft first; it
   is the reason the readvisor exists.)
2. **Did the last answer contradict an earlier one?** → Follow the
   contradiction. Do not resolve it yourself, and do not treat it as a
   mistake: *"Earlier you said you cut polish first. Just now you said the
   last thing you'd give up is the ending. When does each apply?"*
   Contradictions are where the real shape is.
3. **Was the last answer given with obvious energy** — long, specific, a
   story, a phrase they clearly like? → Stay on that axis and go deeper.
   People tell you what matters to them by how they answer.
4. **Have you asked two questions in a row of the same flavour?** → Change
   flavour. Forced choice, then free response, then a scenario. A run of four
   lettered questions makes the session feel like a form.
5. **Is craft still under two answers?** → Ask a second craft question.
6. **Otherwise** → take the axis with the fewest answers and ask there.

Once six axes are `[~]` or better and the first three are `[x]`, switch to
the stress questions (`question-bank.md` § 10) for the last two or three.

**One or two questions per message. Never a flood.** A message with six
numbered questions in it is a questionnaire, and if that is what the session
needed, you should have written one.

---

## Choosing the questions — questionnaires

There is no next-question loop. You pick the whole slate up front, and you
get one shot, so weight it toward the axes that survive being answered
without you in the room.

A reliable 14-question slate:

| Axis | Questions | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| craft | 3 | Written for this brief. Put them first — they are the ones the person will enjoy. |
| method | 2 | One forced choice, one free. |
| risk | 1 | |
| failure | 2 | One forced choice, one free. |
| judgment | 1 | |
| voice | 1 | |
| taste | 2 | One of them the `[RANK]`; people answer it well in writing. |
| limits | 2 | Always include LIMIT-1 and LIMIT-3. |

Stretch to 18 if the brief is broad. Do not go past 18 — you are asking for
somebody's evening.

Leave the stress questions out, or include at most one. A self-rebuttal
question with nobody there to follow up is just confusing.

---

## When an answer is ambiguous

Probe **once**, then move on. Useful probes:

- "What would have to be true for the other answer to be right?"
- "Is that more about the work or more about the people?"
- "Say more about why?"
- "If you had to put a number on it?"

If it is still unclear after one probe, mark the axis `[~]`, move on, and
remember it. Some axes never crystallise. The finished readvisor reflects
that honestly — which is better than a confident invention.

---

## When to stop

Stop at the first of these:

- 18 questions asked.
- The first three axes are `[x]`, six axes are `[~]` or better, and the last
  three answers have not surprised you.
- The person is tiring: short answers, "I don't know really", asking how many
  are left.
- They say they are done.

Then go to the follow-up pass (`followups.md`). Do not go straight to
synthesis, ever.

If the preview later reveals a hole, it is completely fine to come back and
ask one or two more questions. That is not a failure of the interview; it is
what the preview is for.

## A short trace

The brief: *"a readvisor that tells me whether a draft is finished."* The
source is the user's former editor, by questionnaire. These are the live
follow-ups after the answers came back — the same checklist, applied to a
short conversation with the user rather than a long one with the source.

    Map after reading the returned answers:
      [x] craft     three answers, one of them quotable
      [x] method    "I read the ending first"
      [~] limits    LIMIT-1 answered, LIMIT-3 skipped
      [~] failure   one forced choice, no why
      [ ] risk      not asked
      [~] judgment  one answer
      [x] voice     two answers, very characteristic
      [~] taste     the ranking, answered fast
      [ ] conflict  not asked

    Rule 1 fires: limits is not [x], and limits is load-bearing.
    → Ask the user: "She skipped the question about what to hand back.
      From what you know of her, what would she refuse to have an
      opinion about?"

    Answer names two things. limits → [x].

    Rule 2: no contradiction.
    Rule 3: the user answered with energy, and quoted her twice.
    → Stay there: "You quoted her twice just now. Which of those two
      would she say more often?"

    Now: three load-bearing axes [x], six axes at [~] or better.
    Risk and conflict are still empty — and for a readvisor about
    finished drafts, they may simply not matter. Note them as honest
    silences and go to synthesis.

That last move is the one to copy. Empty axes are only a problem when they
are load-bearing for *this* brief. A readvisor about drafts does not need a
theory of organisational conflict, and pretending otherwise is how a profile
turns into a horoscope.
