# Structure — the shapes a brain dump can take

Read this when you are about to name the groups in an outline. It gives you
the vocabulary for three shapes — **story**, **argument**, **plan** — and two
complete worked examples.

Everything here is in service of one decision: *what are the columns?*

---

## Step zero — which shape is this?

Read the brain dump once and ask what the material is trying to do.

| The material… | Shape | Columns are usually |
|---|---|---|
| Things happen, to someone, in an order | **story** | acts, or parts, or movements |
| Something is being claimed, and defended | **argument** | claim, grounds, counter-case, close |
| Something is going to be built or done | **plan** | phases |

Mixed material is common. A memoir is a story with an argument in it. A
proposal is a plan with an argument in front of it. Pick the shape that
carries the *spine* — the thing that falls apart if you remove it — and let
the other shape live inside one column.

When you genuinely cannot tell, that is one of your two questions.

---

# Part 1 — story shapes

## Arc

An **arc** is a line of change: someone or something is one way at the start
and another way at the end, and the middle is the crossing. A piece has at
least one. It may have three.

- **One arc** — a short story, most essays-in-story-clothes, a single
  episode. Columns are acts or movements of that one arc.
- **Two or three arcs** — a novel with a plot arc and a relationship arc; a
  documentary with a subject's arc and the maker's arc. Give each arc its own
  column, or give each arc its own *row* of cards inside the act columns —
  whichever the material makes easier to read.
- **Many arcs** — a collection, a season, an anthology. Then the composure is
  usually `cards` form, one group per piece, and each piece gets its own
  scaffold later.

Name the arc by its change, not its topic: `From apprentice to rival`, not
`The workshop`.

## Beats

A **beat** is one unit of change — the smallest thing that happens after
which the situation is different. One beat, one card.

A beat is not a scene, a chapter, or a paragraph. A scene can hold three
beats; three scenes can deliver one beat. Write cards for beats and let the
writer decide how to chapter them.

Good beat cards:

- **Named by what changes.** `She admits she can't read the charts`, not
  `Scene at the table`.
- **Short body.** Two or four lines: what happens, who is present, what is
  different afterwards.
- **In order within the column.** Top to bottom is time.

A column with fourteen beats is telling you the act is really two acts. A
column with one beat is telling you the act is really a transition.

## Continuity threads

A **continuity thread** is something that must stay consistent across the
whole piece and belongs to no single beat: a wound that has to keep hurting,
a season that has to keep turning, a rule of the world, a motif, a lie
somebody has to keep telling, a second language somebody speaks.

Threads are where structures actually break. Give them a column of their own,
named `Threads` or `Continuity`, with one card per thread:

    ### The lamp oil

    Runs out on a schedule. Named in act one, low in act two, gone at the
    turn. If any beat moves, check this card.

A thread card's body should say *where it is touched* and *what breaks if it
is forgotten*. It is the writer's own tripwire.

## Denouement and endings — and why "not always plural of each"

These four words get used loosely. Keep them apart:

- **Climax** — the beat where the arc's question is answered. Usually one.
- **Denouement** — what settles after that answer: consequence, aftermath,
  the quiet page. A piece may have one, or none at all. Short forms often cut
  straight from climax to last line.
- **Ending** — the last thing the reader receives. Usually one.
- **Endings, plural** — real when the piece has several arcs that land
  separately (each arc gets its ending), when the form is a collection, or
  when the writer is genuinely choosing between two candidate endings and
  wants both on the canvas.

So: **count them before you name the group.** Do not write a group called
`Endings` because it sounds structural. Ask the material how many it has.

- One arc, one landing → group named `Ending`.
- Three arcs landing in three places → `Endings`, three cards.
- A long quiet aftermath → a `Denouement` group *and* an `Ending` card.
- Two candidate last scenes → `Endings`, two cards, and a gap card asking
  which one the piece is actually for.

In the `scaffold` form, a group named `ending`, `endings`, `denouement` or
`resolution` is laid out along the bottom, under the act columns. That is
why the names matter.

## A story skeleton that usually works

    # <title>
    ## Premise            (one card: the logline)
    ## Act one            (beats)
    ## Act two            (beats)
    ## Act three          (beats)
    ## Threads            (one card per continuity thread)
    ## Ending             (denouement and/or last beat)

Five acts, two acts, or four movements are all fine. Use the material's own
divisions when it has them.

# Part 2 — argument shapes

An essay, a review, a proposal's case, a talk, a manifesto, a memo that is
trying to change someone's mind.

The five parts:

| Part | What it is | The card asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The thing being asserted. One sentence. | What are you saying? |
| **Grounds** | The evidence, examples, data, experience. | What makes you say it? |
| **Warrant** | Why the grounds support the claim — the assumption in the middle. Usually unwritten, always load-bearing. | Why does that evidence mean that? |
| **Counter-case** | The strongest version of the other side, stated fairly. | Who is right to disagree, and about what? |
| **Close** | What the reader should now think, feel or do. | So what? |

Notes a small model should keep in mind:

- **The warrant is where gaps live.** A brain dump almost never states it.
  When you cannot name the warrant, write a gap card: `[gap: why does the
  1970s example bear on the present case?]`. This is the single most useful
  card this skill produces for non-fiction.
- **Grounds usually want several cards**, one per kind of evidence. Put them
  in order of strength, strongest first, and say in the body which ones the
  argument can survive losing.
- **A counter-case group with no cards** means the writer has not met their
  opposition yet. Gap card.
- **Close is not summary.** If the only close in the dump is "and that's why
  it matters", that is a gap card too.

Skeleton:

    # <title>
    ## Thesis             (the claim, one card)
    ## Grounds            (one card per body of evidence)
    ## Warrants           (the assumptions doing the work)
    ## Counter-case       (the best opposing reading, and the reply)
    ## Close              (what changes for the reader)

`Thesis` at the top spans the composure in the `scaffold` form, the same way
`Premise` does for a story.

# Part 3 — plan shapes

A project, a build, a move, a launch, a course, a research program.

The five parts:

| Part | What it is | The card asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | The outcome, in the world, not the work. | What is true afterwards that is not true now? |
| **Phases** | Ordered stages of work. One group per phase. | What happens, in what order? |
| **Dependencies** | What a phase needs before it can start — a person, a decision, a file, money, weather, somebody else's answer. | What are we waiting on? |
| **Risks** | What could make this not work, and the early sign of each. | What would we see first if this were going wrong? |
| **Done-when** | The test that ends each phase, and the one that ends the plan. | How do we know to stop? |

Notes:

- **A phase with no done-when is not a phase**, it is a hope. Write the
  done-when card, or a gap card asking for it.
- **Dependencies belong on the canvas, not in prose.** One card per
  dependency, named by what is awaited and from whom.
- **Risks are not fears.** A risk card names an observable early sign. "The
  translation might be bad" is a fear; "the first chapter comes back and we
  disagree about the register" is a risk.

Skeleton:

    # <title>
    ## Goal
    ## Phase 1 — <name>   (work cards + a done-when card)
    ## Phase 2 — <name>
    ## Phase 3 — <name>
    ## Dependencies
    ## Risks

# Worked example A — a story

## The brain dump (what the user pasted)

> ok so the idea is a woman in her fifties inherits her father's
> lighthouse, on an island, and the lighthouse has been automated for
> twenty years so there's no job there, but the island council still pays a
> tiny stipend to whoever "keeps" it, which is basically a pension for
> nobody. she comes back to sell it. there's a whole thing with her brother
> who stayed. he's angry. the lamp itself is the thing i keep coming back
> to — the original lens is still in there, brass, enormous, and there's a
> collector who wants it, that's the money. she's a translator by trade,
> that matters somehow, she's spent her whole life carrying things between
> languages and never keeping any of it. the brother has a kid. the kid
> likes her immediately which annoys everyone. i know she doesn't sell it. i
> don't know why she doesn't sell it. maybe a storm? feels cheap. the
> ending i can see is her up in the lamp room at night doing her actual
> work, translating, with the light going around above her. that's the
> image. also somewhere the mother should come up, she left the island when
> they were small and nobody says her name.

## What to notice before writing anything

- **One arc, clearly**: come to sell → does not sell. The change is hers.
- **A second arc**: the brother. Smaller, real, lands separately. Give it
  cards inside the act columns, not its own column — the dump does not carry
  enough for a column.
- **Threads**: the lens, the mother's name, the translating.
- **The ending is known, the turn is not.** The user says so themselves: *"I
  don't know why she doesn't sell it."* That is the gap. Do not solve it.
  Writing "a storm destroys the buyer's boat" would be inventing material —
  and the user has already rejected a storm.
- **Keep her phrase**: "carrying things between languages and never keeping
  any of it."

## The outline

    # The Keeper's Stipend

    ## Premise

    ### Logline

    A translator comes home to sell the lighthouse nobody needs, and
    keeps it instead.

    ## Act one — the inventory

    ### She arrives to sell

    Flies in with a folder, a valuation, and a return date. Treats the
    island as a transaction she is completing on someone else's behalf —
    which is what she does for a living.

    ### The brother has stayed

    He kept the island. He is angry in the specific way of someone whose
    staying was never chosen, only defaulted into.

    ### The kid attaches to her instantly

    And it annoys everyone, including her.

    ### The collector names a number

    The brass lens is the only real money in the building. The offer makes
    selling feel like a decision rather than a formality.

    ## Act two — the stipend

    ### She starts doing her own work in the lamp room

    First time in the piece she is translating for herself and not between
    other people.

    ### The brother's case

    He does not want the money. He wants the fact of the place. His version
    of their childhood is not hers.

    ### The mother's absence surfaces

    Someone almost says her name. Nobody does.

    ### [gap: why does she not sell?]

    The end state is known and the ending image exists. The turn that gets
    her there is missing, and the notes rule out the obvious one ("maybe a
    storm? feels cheap"). Everything in act three rests on this card.

    ## Act three — the keeping

    ### She refuses the collector

    Whatever the reason on the gap card turns out to be, this is where it
    becomes an act.

    ### The brother and the stipend

    His arc lands here: what he gets is not the building.

    ## Threads

    ### The brass lens

    The original Fresnel lens, still in the tower. It is the money, the
    inheritance, and the image. Touched in act one (valuation), act two
    (she works beneath it), act three (she refuses to sell it). If any beat
    moves, this card moves with it.

    ### Nobody says the mother's name

    She left when they were small. The rule is that the name is never
    spoken aloud in the piece. Breaking the rule on purpose, once, is
    available — but it has to be on purpose.

    ### Translation as the life

    Her trade is the theme: "carrying things between languages and never
    keeping any of it." Every act should touch her working, at least once,
    so the last image reads as a change and not as decoration.

    ## Ending

    ### The lamp room at night

    She is up in the tower doing her own translating, the light turning
    above her. The user has this image already; it is the last card, not a
    beat to be invented around.

Then:

    <title>The Keeper's Stipend</title>
    <form>scaffold</form>
    <content>
    …the outline above, in full…
    </content>
    </tool>

## What to say afterwards

> Fifteen cards: premise on top, three act columns, a continuity column, and
> the ending along the bottom. One gap, and it is the one you named
> yourself — *why doesn't she sell?* Everything in act three hangs off that
> card, so it is the next thing worth an hour.
>
> I didn't place two things. The brother's kid only appears in act one; if
> the kid is load-bearing, they need a beat in act two. And "the mother
> should come up somewhere" is a thread card rather than a scene — say the
> word and I'll give her a beat instead.

# Worked example B — an essay

## The brain dump

> I want to write something about why local-first software feels different
> to use and it's not about privacy. everyone writes the privacy essay. the
> thing I actually notice is latency and permanence — the app opens
> instantly and the file is still there next year. my evidence is mostly
> just my own use, a decade of notes apps that died, plus the obvious ones,
> and there's that essay from the Ink & Switch people that named the whole
> category. counterargument is obviously collaboration, which cloud does
> better, and honestly sync is genuinely hard, I don't want to pretend it
> isn't. I keep wanting to say something about how it changes what you're
> willing to put in a file but I can't make it land. ending should be
> something like: the question isn't who can see your notes, it's whether
> your notes will see you again in ten years.

## What to notice

- **The claim is sharp and it is a negation**: not privacy — latency and
  permanence. Lead with it.
- **Grounds are thin and the user knows it**: personal use, dead apps, one
  named piece of prior art. Say so in the card bodies rather than padding.
- **The warrant is unstated**: why does *my* decade of dead notes apps bear
  on *your* software choices? That is the gap.
- **The counter-case is already honest** ("sync is genuinely hard"). Keep
  their concession; it is the strongest thing in the dump.
- **The close is written already**, in their own words. Do not paraphrase it.
- **One idea will not land** ("what you're willing to put in a file"). That
  is a gap card, not a cut.

## The outline

    # Not the Privacy Essay

    ## Thesis

    ### The claim

    Local-first software feels different for reasons that have nothing to
    do with privacy. The difference is latency and permanence: it opens
    now, and it will still open later.

    ## Grounds

    ### A decade of dead notes apps

    The strongest evidence available here is durational and personal. Name
    the pattern — services ending, formats stranded, exports that lost the
    thing that mattered. The argument can survive losing any single
    example but not the pattern.

    ### Latency you can feel

    Instant open, instant search, no spinner, no session. Small, constant,
    and hard to argue with because the reader can test it in the time it
    takes to read the sentence.

    ### The prior art

    The Ink & Switch essay that named the category. Use it to place the
    argument, not to prove it — this is context, and one citation is not
    grounds.

    ## Warrants

    ### [gap: why does one person's decade generalize?]

    The claim is about how software feels to use; the evidence is one
    person's history with it. The assumption in between is unstated and
    it is the load-bearing one. Options the notes do not choose between:
    the pattern is common enough to be assumed, or the piece narrows to a
    first-person essay and stops claiming generality.

    ### Permanence is a property of the format, not the vendor

    This one the notes do support, and it is worth stating outright: a
    file that opens in anything outlives the company that made it.

    ## Counter-case

    ### Collaboration is genuinely better in the cloud

    State it at full strength — multiplayer editing, shared links, no
    setup. The notes already concede this; the concession is the most
    persuasive part of the piece and should not be softened.

    ### Sync is genuinely hard

    Keep the honesty: "I don't want to pretend it isn't." The reply, if
    there is one, is about what you get for the difficulty, not a denial
    of it.

    ### [gap: does the counter-case get a reply, or does it stand?]

    An essay is allowed to leave the strongest objection standing. The
    notes do not say which this piece is doing, and the close reads
    differently depending on the answer.

    ## Unplaced

    ### [gap: "what you're willing to put in a file"]

    Named twice in the notes and never landed. It may be the real thesis
    wearing a smaller hat, or a second essay. Worth ten minutes before it
    gets cut.

    ## Close

    ### The last line already exists

    "The question isn't who can see your notes, it's whether your notes
    will see you again in ten years." In the writer's own words. Do not
    paraphrase it; build the last paragraph toward it.

Then the same tool call, with `form` still `scaffold` — `Thesis` spans the
top, `Close` sits along the bottom.

## What to say afterwards

> Six columns and twelve cards. Three gaps: the warrant (why your decade
> generalizes — that is the real one), whether the counter-case gets a reply
> or is allowed to stand, and the "what you're willing to put in a file"
> idea, which I could not attach to any column. I gave it a card of its own
> rather than dropping it.
>
> Your last line is on the canvas verbatim, and the grounds are ordered
> strongest-first, with a note in each body about what the argument survives
> losing.

# A plan, in brief

A full plan outline follows the same grammar. The fragment shows the
vocabulary in place:

    # Six Languages by March

    ## Goal

    ### What is true in March

    Every interface string and both manuals read naturally in six
    languages, checked by someone who speaks each one.

    ## Phase 1 — extract

    ### Pull every string into the catalogs

    ### Done when

    No English literal remains outside a catalog, and the check script is
    green.

    ## Phase 2 — translate

    ### First pass per language

    ### [gap: who reads the Japanese?]

    Every other language has a reader named in the notes. This one does
    not, and phase 3 cannot close without them.

    ## Dependencies

    ### A reader per language

    Named for five of six. See the gap card in phase 2.

    ## Risks

    ### Register drift

    Early sign: the first reviewed chapter comes back with the reviewer
    and the writer disagreeing about formality rather than accuracy.

# Reminders

- Groups are columns. Cards are beats, grounds, or work.
- Count the arcs and the endings; do not assume either is plural.
- The warrant and the done-when are the two places gaps hide.
- Every card traces to the dump, or it is a gap card.
- Keep the user's own phrasing wherever it is better than yours. It usually
  is — they have been living with this material and you have not.
