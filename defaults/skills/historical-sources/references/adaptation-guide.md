## 1. When Adaptation Is Required

Adaptation is required when:
- The source text was originally published after 1928 (copyright)
- The translation of a public domain text was published after 1928
- You're unsure of the copyright status
- A modern editor has substantially restructured or modernized an older text

Adaptation is NOT required when:
- The original text is pre-1929 and in English
- You're using a pre-1929 translation
- The source is a US government document
- You're producing a fresh translation from an original-language source

When in doubt: adapt. A well-adapted passage serves the novelist just as well as a verbatim one.

## 2. The Adaptation Spectrum

Think of adaptation as existing on a spectrum:

```
VERBATIM ←——————————————————————→ RECONSTRUCTION
   |         |           |              |
   |    Light edit   Substantial    Fully new
   |   (spelling,    reworking     text in period
   |    obvious     (structure,      style
   |    errors)      phrasing,
   |                 vocabulary)
   |
   ↑ Use only for
     confirmed PD
```

For copyrighted sources, you must be at "substantial reworking" or beyond. Light editing is not adaptation.

## 3. Technique: Substance Transfer

The core adaptation technique. Think of it as: absorb the historical content completely, then re-express it as if you were a literate person from that period writing about the same events.

### Step 1: Extract the Facts
Read the copyrighted passage and identify:
- What specific events, objects, or actions are described
- What emotions or attitudes are expressed
- What sensory details are present
- What social dynamics are at play
- What period-specific vocabulary or concepts appear

### Step 2: Forget the Sentences
Deliberately set aside the author's sentence structures, word choices, and organizational logic. You're keeping the *information*, not the *prose*.

### Step 3: Rebuild from Period Models
Using vocabulary, syntax, and idiom drawn from public domain sources of the same period and region, construct new sentences that convey the same substance.

Key question: "If a literate person from this exact time and place sat down to describe these same events, how would they write it?"

### Step 4: Cross-Check
Compare your adaptation against the original. If more than 5 consecutive words match, rework that section. The substance should be recognizable; the prose should not be.

## 4. Technique: Register Matching

Different kinds of historical documents have distinctive registers. When adapting, match the register of the document type, not of the modern author.

### Personal Letters
- Direct address, intimate or formal depending on relationship
- Sentence fragments, self-corrections, asides
- Practical detail mixed with emotion
- Period-specific salutations and closings
- Often rambling, associative rather than structured
- Model: Paston Letters, Pliny's Letters, Cicero's correspondence

### Official Documents
- Formulaic openings and closings
- Legal or bureaucratic phrasing
- Third person or royal we
- Precision about titles, dates, quantities
- Conditional clauses and qualifications
- Model: State papers, royal charters, papal bulls

### Chronicle / Historical Narrative
- Sequential, often annalistic (year by year)
- Minimal emotional editorializing (or heavy moralizing, depending on tradition)
- Formulaic transitions: "In this year..." "At that time..."
- Attribution of motive often simplistic: greed, piety, treachery
- Model: Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Froissart, Bede

### Testimony / Deposition
- Question-and-answer format or continuous narrative
- Specific about sensory detail (what was seen, heard)
- Repetitive, circling back to key moments
- Social deference or defiance toward questioners
- Model: Inquisition records, trial transcripts

### Religious / Liturgical
- Elevated, rhythmic, often repetitive
- Biblical allusion and quotation
- Imperative mood (prayers, commands)
- Abstract nouns personified
- Model: Book of Common Prayer, Vulgate, relevant sacred texts

## 5. Technique: Period Voice Construction

When you need to construct period-appropriate prose (for adaptation or reconstruction), use this process:

### Step 1: Establish the Vocabulary Field
Search for word lists, glossaries, and dictionaries specific to the period:
- `"[period] English vocabulary"` or `"[period] glossary"`
- `"[century] common words"` or `"[profession] terminology [period]"`
- The Oxford English Dictionary's date-of-first-use is invaluable — avoid words not attested in the target period

### Step 2: Establish Syntactic Patterns
Read 2-3 public domain sources from the same period and note:
- Average sentence length
- Subordinate clause patterns (front-loaded? trailing?)
- Connective words (and, but, therefore, moreover, howbeit, notwithstanding)
- How they handle lists, descriptions, temporal sequences
- Paragraph structure (or lack thereof)

### Step 3: Absorb the Music
Before writing, read a substantial passage from a period source aloud (or silently, attending to rhythm). Let the cadence settle into your prose. Period writing has distinct rhythms that signal authenticity even to readers who can't identify the specific markers.

### Step 4: Write, Then Prune Anachronisms
Write the adapted or reconstructed passage, then review for:
- Words not in use during the target period
- Modern sentence patterns (especially short declarative sentences strung together)
- Modern emotional vocabulary
- Concepts that didn't exist yet (nationalism before nations, individualism before the concept, etc.)

## 6. Working Examples

### Example: Adapting a Copyrighted Translation of a Medieval Chronicle

**The situation**: You need a passage from a 13th-century chronicle. The original is in Latin (public domain) but the only accessible English translation is from 2003 (copyrighted).

**The copyrighted translation says something like**:
"The sultan's army arrived before the walls at dawn. The defenders, already weakened by months of siege, could do little more than watch from the battlements as the massive force arranged itself in battle formation."

**A bad adaptation** (too close):
"The sultan's forces reached the walls at daybreak. The defenders, weakened by the long siege, could only watch from the walls as the great army formed up for battle."

This just swaps a few words. It's still clearly derived from the same sentence structures.

**A good adaptation** (substance transfer with period register):
"At first light came the host of the sultan before the city, a great multitude arrayed for war. Those within the walls, much diminished by the privations of the siege, had no strength remaining to do more than look upon the assembling of their enemies from the high places."

This conveys the same information but the prose is built from period patterns (front-loaded temporal clause, "host" for army, "privations" instead of "months of siege," passive observation described differently).

### Example: Adapting a Copyrighted Eyewitness Account

**The situation**: A 20th-century memoir describes a historical event. The author's words are copyrighted.

**Process**:
1. Extract the facts: what happened, what was seen/heard/felt, in what order
2. Research: what other accounts of this event exist? What period-specific vocabulary applies?
3. Rebuild: write as if you're a different eyewitness — same events, different words, different emphases
4. The adapted version should read as a plausible alternative witness, not as a paraphrase

### Example: English-to-English for Middle English

**Original Middle English**:
"Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote / The droghte of March hath perced to the roote"

**Adaptation for a novel** (not a scholarly translation):
"When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root"

Light touch: spelling modernized, syntax preserved, rhythm maintained. Chaucer is public domain so this could be verbatim, but the principle applies to Middle English texts that might need a gentler touch for modern readers.

## 7. Common Pitfalls

### The Synonym Swap
Replacing individual words while keeping identical sentence structure. This is NOT adaptation. It's detectable and potentially infringing.

### The Modern Ventriloquist
Writing "period" prose that actually sounds like a modern novelist's idea of how old people talked. Symptoms: excessive "thee" and "thou," melodramatic phrasing, unnaturally formal diction applied uniformly regardless of speaker or document type.

### The Flattener
Taking rich, complex period prose and simplifying it into modern journalistic sentences. Period documents are often syntactically complex because the writers *thought* in complex structures. Preserve that complexity.

### The Over-Explainer
Adding modern interpretive framework to period text. If a medieval writer says "God punished the city for its sins," don't adapt this as "The populace, following the common medieval belief in divine retribution, interpreted the disaster as punishment for sin." Let the voice remain its own.

### The Vocabulary Anachronist
Using words that didn't exist in the target period. "Interesting" before the 17th century. "Sentimental" before the 18th. "Traumatized" before the 20th. Check dates of first attestation for any word that feels potentially modern.

## 8. The Reconstruction Protocol

When no source text survives — when you know a conversation happened, a letter was written, a speech was given, but the words are lost — reconstruction is the last resort.

### Requirements for Reconstruction

1. **Historical basis**: There must be evidence that the communication existed (references in other sources, logical necessity given known events)
2. **Period models**: You must have read actual sources from the same period, region, and document type to model your prose on
3. **Documented vocabulary**: Every significant word should be attested in the target period
4. **Explicit marking**: The output must be clearly labeled as reconstruction

### Reconstruction Process

1. **Research the gap**: What do we know about the lost communication? Who produced it, when, to whom, about what?
2. **Find the nearest models**: Identify 2-3 surviving documents of the same type from the same period and region
3. **Extract the patterns**: Note vocabulary, syntax, formulae, conventions, rhetorical strategies
4. **Build the reconstruction**: Write the text using those patterns, incorporating known historical facts
5. **Document your models**: In the source tag, list the specific documents you modeled the prose on

### Marking Reconstructions

```
═══════════════════════════════════════════
[SOURCE TYPE: RECONSTRUCTION]
[BASIS: Historical references to this letter in Chronicle X and Document Y]
[MODELS: Prose style based on [specific PD source 1] and [specific PD source 2]]
[VOCABULARY: All significant terms attested pre-[date] per OED / period glossary]
[NOTE: No surviving text. This is a plausible period-style invention, not a real source.]
═══════════════════════════════════════════
```

The novelist should know they're working with invented material. The goal is to give them *plausible* period prose, not to deceive them into thinking it's real. Transparency here is non-negotiable.
