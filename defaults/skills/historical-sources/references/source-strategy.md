## 1. The Source Hierarchy

Not all historical text is equally useful for fiction. Prefer sources in this order:

### Tier A — Voices of Individuals
The most novelistically valuable sources are those where a specific person speaks in their own voice:
- **Personal letters** — Intimate, unguarded, full of texture
- **Diaries and journals** — Interior monologue from history
- **Testimony** — Court transcripts, depositions, inquisition records
- **Petitions and complaints** — People asking for things reveal what they lack
- **Memoirs and autobiographies** — Written after the fact but in personal voice

### Tier B — Official and Public Voices
Useful for authority, ceremony, and the public register:
- **Speeches and orations** — Public rhetoric of the period
- **Laws, edicts, decrees** — The voice of power
- **Diplomatic correspondence** — Formal but often revealing
- **Religious texts, sermons, prayers** — The spiritual vocabulary of daily life
- **Treaties and agreements** — The language of negotiation

### Tier C — Documentary Texture
Not voices exactly, but the stuff of daily life rendered in words:
- **Inventories and wills** — What people owned, what they valued
- **Merchant records and price lists** — The economy made concrete
- **Travel accounts** — Outsiders describing what they see
- **Medical texts and recipes** — How people understood bodies and food
- **Technical manuals** — How things were made and done

### Tier D — Chronicles and Histories
Written by observers, not participants, but still period language:
- **Contemporary chronicles** — Monks, scholars, court historians
- **Annals and year-books** — Terse but factual
- **Biographical accounts** — Lives of notables written near their time

### Tier E — Literary and Cultural Sources
Period voice but with artistic shaping:
- **Poetry, songs, ballads** — The emotional register of an era
- **Proverbs and sayings** — Common wisdom, common speech
- **Drama and dialogue** — How people imagined speech sounding
- **Inscriptions and graffiti** — The most casual and ephemeral voices

| HathiTrust | hathitrust.org | Digitized books | All periods |

### By Period

**Ancient (pre-500 CE)**:
- Perseus for Greek/Latin
- Sacred Texts for religious documents
- Livius.org for ancient history sources
- Attalus.org for Hellenistic sources

**Medieval (500-1500)**:
- Fordham Medieval Sourcebook is the single best starting point
- TEAMS Middle English Texts (d.lib.rochester.edu)
- Internet Medieval Sourcebook has translated excerpts organized by topic
- Epistolae: Medieval Women's Letters (epistolae.ctl.columbia.edu)

**Early Modern (1500-1800)**:
- Early English Books Online
- State Papers Online (for English government)
- Gallica (gallica.bnf.fr) for French sources
- Google Books for 18th century

**Modern (1800-present)**:
- Internet Archive (by far the richest)
- Google Books (pre-1929 full access)
- National archives of relevant countries
- Newspaper archives (many digitized)

### Search Heuristics

When you don't know where a source might be:

1. **Search for the document by title** — If you know a chronicle, letter collection, or speech exists, search for it by name + "full text" or "transcription" or "translation"
2. **Search for published editions** — Many primary sources were published in 19th-century scholarly editions that are now in the public domain. Search: `"[document] edited by" OR "translated by" site:archive.org`
3. **Follow the footnotes** — When you find a secondary source discussing a primary text, look at its citations. The cited editions are often digitized.
4. **Check the language versions** — Wikisource exists in many languages. A source might not be on English Wikisource but might be on Latin, French, German, Italian, or Arabic Wikisource.
5. **Look for anthology volumes** — Search: `"[period] [region] sources" OR "[period] documents translated"`. Many excellent source anthologies were published in the 19th-20th centuries and are digitized.

## 3. Copyright and Public Domain Rules

### The Core Rule

**Texts written before 1929**: Almost certainly in the public domain worldwide. Use verbatim.

**Translations**: A medieval text is public domain but a 2015 translation of it is copyrighted. A 1890 translation is public domain. Always check when the translation was published, not when the original was written.

### Practical Guidelines

| Situation | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| Original text pre-1929, in English | Verbatim — public domain |
| Original text pre-1929, translated pre-1929 | Verbatim — both public domain |
| Original text pre-1929, translated post-1928 | Adapt the translation, or find an older one, or translate fresh |
| Original text post-1928 | Adapt — the text is under copyright |
| Modern scholarly edition of ancient text | The text is PD but editorial apparatus (footnotes, introductions) may not be |
| Government documents (US) | Generally public domain regardless of date |
| Speeches delivered publicly | The speech itself may be copyrighted by the speaker/estate |

### When Uncertain

Treat as copyrighted and adapt. It's always safe to adapt. Mark the source clearly so the user can make their own determination.

### What "Adaptation" Means

Adaptation is NOT:
- Changing a few words
- Swapping synonyms
- Rearranging sentence order while keeping the same structure

Adaptation IS:
- Absorbing the information and substance
- Re-expressing it in period-appropriate language
- Preserving the emotional register and historical specifics
- Producing a new text that conveys the same meaning but isn't derived recognizably from any single modern author's rendering

See `adaptation-guide.md` for detailed protocols.

## 4. Search Methodology Deep Dive

### Phase 1: Establish What Exists

Before searching for text, establish what primary sources survive from the period/event:

1. Search: `"[event/period] primary sources"` or `"[event] contemporary accounts"`
2. Look for "Sources and Further Reading" sections in encyclopedia entries
3. Identify the KEY DOCUMENTS — every period has its landmark texts
4. Note which languages they're in and whether English translations exist

### Phase 2: Find the Key Documents

For each identified source:

1. Search for it by exact title + "full text" or "online" or "digital"
2. Check Internet Archive, Google Books, Wikisource
3. If not found in English, check if a pre-1929 translation exists
4. If only modern translations exist, fetch the original language version if possible

### Phase 3: Mine the Found Sources

Once you have a full text:

1. Read/scan for the most vivid, novelistically useful passages
2. Extract passages that contain: specific detail, emotional expression, sensory information, period vocabulary, human voice
3. Favor passages where someone is speaking to or about a specific person or event
4. Avoid passages that are pure lists, genealogies, or dry chronology (unless that's what the user needs)

### Phase 4: Fill the Gaps

After extracting from major sources:

1. Search for minor sources: letters from ordinary people, court records, mercantile documents
2. Look for material that provides contrasting perspectives (the conqueror and the conquered, the official and the private)
3. Seek sensory and domestic detail: what people ate, wore, feared, prayed for
4. Find the edges: graffiti, marginalia, complaints, jokes

## 5. Source Classification System

Every passage delivered must carry a classification tag block. Use this exact format:

```
═══════════════════════════════════════════
[SOURCE TYPE: VERBATIM]
[PROVENANCE: Nicetas Choniates, "O City of Byzantium" (Historia), c. 1206]
[FOUND AT: Medieval Sourcebook, Fordham University]
[ORIGINAL LANGUAGE: Greek — using 1984 Magoulias translation, adapted]
[COPYRIGHT: Original PD; translation copyrighted — text has been adapted]
[ADAPTATION: Sentence structures reorganized; modern scholarly phrasings replaced with period-appropriate equivalents; substance preserved]
═══════════════════════════════════════════
```

Allowed SOURCE TYPE values:
- **VERBATIM** — Exact transcription from a public domain source
- **TRANSLATION** — Fresh translation from an original-language source
- **ADAPTATION** — Copyrighted source substantially reworked
- **RECONSTRUCTION** — No surviving source; period-style invented text

## 6. Working with Non-English Sources

### Translation Principles for Fiction

The goal is NOT a scholarly translation. The goal is a literary translation that could live in a novel — that sounds like a person from this period speaking, not like a modern academic explaining what they said.

**Preserve:**
- Sentence rhythm and length patterns (don't break long sentences into short modern ones)
- Emotional vocabulary (if the original uses five words for grief, find five English words)
- Forms of address and social hierarchy in speech
- Religious and cultural references without over-explaining them
- Period-appropriate register (a king speaks differently from a merchant)

**Avoid:**
- Modern idioms ("at the end of the day," "game-changer," "moving forward")
- Flattened syntax (turning complex period sentences into simple subject-verb-object)
- Over-translation of cultural concepts (leave some things resonant and unfamiliar)
- Imposing modern emotional frameworks ("trauma," "toxic," "boundaries")

**Allow:**
- Archaisms that add flavor without obscuring meaning (forsooth is too much; forthwith is fine)
- Slightly unusual syntax that signals "this is translated" without being unreadable
- Untranslated terms with contextual glossing
- Deliberate formality where the original is formal

### English-to-English Adaptation

When the source is in archaic English (Old English, Middle English, Early Modern English), the job is different from translation:

- **Old English (pre-1100)**: Essentially a foreign language. Translate as above.
- **Middle English (1100-1500)**: Partially intelligible. Modernize spelling and the most opaque vocabulary, but preserve distinctive syntax and period words that a modern reader can figure out from context.
- **Early Modern English (1500-1700)**: Mostly intelligible. Light touch: regularize spelling, gloss truly obscure terms, otherwise leave alone.
- **18th-19th century English**: Usually needs no adaptation. Use verbatim.
